Membrane repair, immune resolution, and the biology of tissue survival.

The Ma Laboratory studies how cells sense injury and either repair it or amplify it — through the membrane-repair protein MG53, the neutrophil trap regulator CitH3, and the calcium-signaling protein MG29. This site is a working reference for new students, lab members, and visitors to get oriented quickly.

Three proteins, one question

How does a cell decide whether an injury heals or spreads?

Each research line below approaches that question from a different angle — a repair protein that reseals torn membranes, a chromatin mark that turns a defensive immune trap into a source of damage, and a calcium-handling protein that links triad-junction structure to cell survival.

Membrane repair

MG53 / TRIM72

A TRIM-family protein that senses exposed phosphatidylserine at a torn cell membrane, self-assembles into a "repair patch," and reseals the injury — in muscle, heart, lung, kidney, cornea, and beyond.

Read the overview →
Immune resolution

CitH3 & NETosis

When neutrophils release DNA-based traps (NETs) to catch pathogens, citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3) is both the mechanism and the collateral damage — a druggable driver of sepsis, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and autoimmune disease.

Read the overview →
Calcium signaling

MG29

A synaptophysin-family protein at the skeletal-muscle triad junction that organizes store-operated calcium entry and, more recently, has emerged as a regulator of cell-cycle and apoptosis pathways in cancer.

Where the biology is headed

From bench discovery to therapeutic candidate

A recurring theme across the lab's work is translation: recombinant human MG53 (rhMG53) and a humanized anti-CitH3 monoclonal antibody (hCitH3-mAb) are both in active preclinical development as protein therapeutics, alongside long-running mechanistic work on MG29 and calcium homeostasis.
