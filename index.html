Membrane repair, immune resolution, and the biology of tissue survival.

The Ma Laboratory studies how cells sense injury and either repair it or amplify it, through the membrane-repair protein MG53, the neutrophil trap regulator CitH3, the calcium-signaling protein MG29, and the TRIC-A/TRIC-B ion channel family. This site is a working reference for new students, lab members, and visitors to get oriented quickly.

Four proteins, one question

How does a cell decide whether an injury heals or spreads?

Each research line below approaches that question from a different angle: a repair protein that reseals torn membranes, a chromatin mark that turns a defensive immune trap into a source of damage, a calcium-handling protein that links triad-junction structure to cell survival, and an ion channel family that keeps calcium release balanced across muscle, heart, and bone.

Membrane repair

MG53 / TRIM72

A TRIM-family protein that senses exposed phosphatidylserine at a torn cell membrane, self-assembles into a "repair patch," and reseals the injury, in muscle, heart, lung, kidney, cornea, and beyond.

Immune resolution

CitH3 & NETosis

When neutrophils release DNA-based traps (NETs) to catch pathogens, citrullinated histone H3 (CitH3) is both the mechanism and the collateral damage, a druggable driver of sepsis, ischemia-reperfusion injury, and autoimmune disease.

Read the overview →
Calcium signaling

MG29

A synaptophysin-family protein at the skeletal-muscle triad junction that organizes store-operated calcium entry and, more recently, has emerged as a regulator of cell-cycle and apoptosis pathways in cancer.

Read the overview →
Ion channel biology

TRIC-A & TRIC-B

A channel family discovered by the lab in 2007 that balances the charge lost during calcium release from internal stores, with roles spanning cardiac rhythm, pulmonary vascular disease, and bone health.

Where the biology is headed

From bench discovery to therapeutic candidate

A recurring theme across the lab's work is translation: recombinant human MG53 (rhMG53) and a humanized anti-CitH3 monoclonal antibody (hCitH3-mAb) are both in active preclinical development as protein therapeutics, alongside long-running mechanistic work on MG29, TRIC-A/TRIC-B, and calcium homeostasis.
