Calcium homeostasis · ion channel biology

TRIC-A & TRIC-B

Trimeric intracellular cation channels. First identified in 2007 by Hiroshi Takeshima and colleagues at Kyoto University, published in Nature, as a new class of counter-ion channels required for efficient calcium release from the sarcoplasmic and endoplasmic reticulum. The Ma laboratory has since defined much of their physiological and pathological function, particularly at the nuclear envelope and in cardiac and vascular tissue.

The one-line version

A pair of channels that let calcium flow out of a cell's internal stores without short-circuiting the process.

When a muscle contracts or a cell signals, calcium is released from internal reservoirs, the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) in muscle and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) elsewhere. That release would rapidly stall the membrane's electrical balance unless a counter-flow of potassium ions moves the other way. TRIC-A and TRIC-B are the channels that provide that counter-ion flux. TRIC-A is concentrated in excitable tissue (skeletal muscle, heart, vascular smooth muscle, and brain), while TRIC-B is expressed ubiquitously. Beyond that original role, the lab's more recent work shows TRIC-A also sits at the nuclear envelope and helps coordinate gene expression and mitochondrial calcium handling during physiological stress.

For the lab

TRIC-A and TRIC-B are homotrimeric, SR/ER-resident monovalent cation channels that permit K+ counter-flux during ryanodine receptor (RyR)- and IP3 receptor-mediated Ca2+ release. TRIC-A also physically interacts with RyR2 through its carboxyl-terminal tail, directly modulating store-overload-induced Ca2+ release (SOICR) in cardiac muscle, independent of its ion-conducting function. More recently, the lab has found TRIC-A enriched at the nuclear envelope of cardiomyocytes, where it associates with chromatin and Lamin A/C, and shown that TRIC-A shapes SR-mitochondrial Ca2+ transfer, linking calcium signaling to transcriptional and metabolic adaptation.

In plain terms

Think of calcium release from internal storage like draining water from a tank. If nothing replaces the charge that leaves with the water, the tank's pressure balance breaks down and the flow stops working properly. TRIC-A and TRIC-B are the vents that let a counter-flow of potassium back in, keeping the system balanced so calcium signaling can keep happening smoothly, in muscle contraction, heart rhythm, and beyond.

Mechanism

From counter-ion flux to a signaling hub

  1. 1

    Counter-ion support. As RyR or IP3R channels release Ca2+ from the SR/ER, TRIC-A and TRIC-B allow K+ to flow the opposite direction, preventing the membrane depolarization that would otherwise choke off further release.

  2. 2

    Direct RyR2 interaction. In cardiac muscle, TRIC-A's C-terminal tail binds RyR2 directly, acting as an accessory protein that suppresses spontaneous, store-overload-induced Ca2+ release rather than simply conducting current.

  3. 3

    Nuclear envelope localization. TRIC-A is enriched at the nuclear envelope of cardiomyocytes and regenerating skeletal muscle, where it associates with chromatin, positioning it to influence gene regulation alongside its role in the SR.

  4. 4

    SR-mitochondrial coupling. TRIC-A shapes how much Ca2+ transfers from the SR to nearby mitochondria; without it, mitochondria take up excess Ca2+ under stress, driving oxidative damage and, over time, fibrosis.

Beyond excitation-contraction coupling

Disease contexts under active investigation

Since the channel family's discovery, the Ma laboratory has worked for nearly two decades to define its physiological and pathological roles. Current lab work, led by Xinyu Zhou, Ph.D., spans:

Heart failure & stress adaptation

TRIC-A loss exaggerates cardiac hypertrophy, fibrosis, and mitochondrial injury after pressure overload or beta-adrenergic stress.

Cardiac arrhythmia

TRIC-A regulates RyR2-dependent store-overload-induced Ca2+ release; human TRIC-A variants are linked to stress-induced arrhythmia and hypertension.

Pulmonary hypertension

A newly identified population of cardiomyocyte-like cells in pulmonary veins relies on TRIC-A signaling; disruption may contribute to pulmonary vascular remodeling.

Osteogenesis imperfecta

Loss-of-function TRIC-B mutations cause a recessive form of "brittle bone disease" (OI type XIV) through disrupted ER calcium handling and impaired collagen secretion.

Bone maintenance & aging

Ongoing work examines whether TRIC-A also contributes to bone health, with aged TRIC-A knockout mice showing greater trabecular bone loss under disuse.

Muscular dystrophy (EDMD)

TRIC-A's presence at the nuclear envelope, and its colocalization with Emerin, raises a possible link to Emery-Dreifuss muscular dystrophy.

Vascular tone & blood pressure

Loss of TRIC-A in vascular smooth muscle disrupts calcium homeostasis, increasing myogenic tone and driving hypertension in mouse models.

Cardiopulmonary complications of OI

Patients with TRIC-B mutations frequently show septal defects and pulmonary hypertension, motivating study of TRIC channels across the heart-lung axis.
